Write down the line, or show the hard cases?

On 23 October you sat three papers: Weak Rows Paper 1, Weak Rows Paper 2 and Representative Mock 3. On all three you lost a question of the same kind: the model flags too much, and the stem lists what really counts. Each time you picked examples, and the answer was to write the list into the prompt. This page shows those three questions in full, plus four more from the same day where a fix from a neighbouring topic was picked. You don't need to remember any of them.

Three topic numbers from the exam guide come up on this page: 4.1 is writing clear criteria into the prompt, 4.2 is giving the model examples, and 5.5 is deciding which results a person checks. The page itself is in four parts, numbered 1 to 4.

The plain question. A model flags far too many things. Do you write down what to flag and what to skip, or do you show it examples?

An everyday example. You hire someone new to check staff expense receipts. Day one you tell them: "flag anything odd." By lunch they have flagged 80 receipts, and 6 are real problems. Coffee bought on a Saturday, a taxi tip and a total rounded to the nearest pound all looked odd to them.

So you show them 3 receipts that really were fraud. The next day they still flag the coffee and the tips: nobody told them those are fine. Then you write on a card: "Flag only: a receipt over £500 with no manager approval, the same receipt claimed twice, a shop not on our supplier list. Ignore: tips, rounding, weekend dates." The flags drop to about the real ones.

The fraud receipts showed what a problem looks like. Only the card said where the line is, and what sits on the safe side of it.

The one idea: when the question tells you what really counts, write it into the prompt: what to flag, and what to skip. Examples of real problems can't cut false flags, because they never show the model what to leave alone. Examples are the fix only when rules were already written and new cases keep slipping past them.

Words to start with

Stem
The question's own text, above the four options.
Prompt
The written instructions given to the model before it does a task.
Flag, false flag
A flag is the model saying "a person should look at this". A false flag is one where the person looks and finds nothing wrong. Too many false flags and people stop reading the flags at all, including the real ones. Precision is the share of flags that are real: 41 real out of 600 flags is low precision.
Criteria
A written list in the prompt that says what to flag and what to skip, by name. "Flag a premium above the quoted amount; ignore a different font or layout" is criteria. "Flag anything serious" is not: it doesn't name anything.
Few-shot examples
Short worked cases written into the prompt. Each one is a piece of the input (a few lines of a document or of code), the right answer for it, and, in the best ones, a sentence on why. They are not extra documents sent for processing; they sit in the prompt beside the instructions. "Few-shot" just means "a few examples".
Score, threshold, calibrate
Some pipelines have the model give each extracted value a score from 0 to 1. A threshold (or cutoff) is the line: values below it go to a person. Calibrating means setting that line from real results: take a set of documents a person already checked by hand, see how often the model was wrong at each score, and put the cutoff at the score below which that field is wrong too often to trust.
Hand-checked set
Documents a person has already checked, value by value. It is the answer key you compare the model's scores against. The exam guide calls it a "labelled set".
Random and stratified sample
A random sample picks items by chance, to measure how often the model is wrong. A stratified sample first splits the items into groups (for example by document type) and samples from each group, so one bad group can't hide inside a good overall average.
Rubric
A written marking guide: rules for what gets which mark or score.

Words that appear in the past questions: a pipeline is a chain of automatic steps that documents pass through. A subagent is one model run that does one job inside a bigger system (a "synthesis subagent" combines the others' results). To extract a value is to pull it out of a document, such as a date or a total. A quota briefing is a report used to set fishing limits, and biomass is the total weight of a fish stock. A policy schedule is the page of an insurance policy listing its price (the premium), its dates and who is covered (the named insured); the certificate is the separate proof-of-cover document. A pull request is a proposed code change that colleagues review. An N+1 query is code that asks the database once per item inside a loop, instead of once for all items; includes is a way to load the related records in advance, so a loop over them asks the database nothing.

Which way scores point: some questions have the model score how odd something is (high = flag it). Others have it score how confident it is in a value (low = send it to a person). For confidence scores, "below the threshold" means "goes to a person". For oddness scores, "above" means "flag it".

Part 1 · A vague line is not the same as criteria

When we reviewed Representative Mock 3 (its question is the first card at the end of Part 2), you asked: "isn't it better to provide 3 examples so the model can make a better judgement?" Behind that is a fair belief: examples are concrete, and an instruction is not. That is true of a vague instruction. It is not true of criteria. Here they are side by side, for the site inspection reports in that question. The criteria text is made up for this page:

Vague: what was tried
Flag any safety concerns.
Focus on serious issues.
The model still has to guess what "serious" means. It guesses the same way as before, so the flags barely change.
Criteria: what fixes it
Flag: roof leaks, exposed live wiring,
missing guard rails.
Skip: weathered cable ties, faded labels,
cosmetic wear.
Each line names a thing. A routine note now has a rule that says skip it.

Criteria are concrete directions too. They are just written as a list instead of shown as cases. So when a stem says a vague line was tried and "changed little", read that as: vague instructions fail. It does not mean writing things down fails. The exam guide says it directly: lines like "be conservative" or "only report high-confidence findings" don't improve precision; specific criteria for what to report and what to skip do.

Representative Mock 3, 23 Oct, question 49

The prompt asks Claude to flag "any safety concerns" in site inspection reports. Reviewers find that 3 of every 4 flags are routine notes such as "cable ties weathered", while 2 real roof-penetration leaks were missed last quarter. Adding "focus on serious issues" to the prompt changed little. What change should you make?

✗ Your pick: AAdd 3 examples of serious defects to the prompt as few-shot examples
✓ Correct: DAdd to the prompt what to flag, such as roof leaks, and what to skip
The other two options (B, C)
  • BRun the same prompt twice per report and keep flags found in both runs
  • CAsk for routine notes in a second list beside the safety flags
Where it went wrong:
  • The problem was too many flags: 3 routine notes for every real one. The 3 examples in your pick are all serious defects. They show the model what a real flag looks like, but nothing in them says "weathered cable ties: skip". The routine notes keep coming.
  • "Focus on serious issues" failed because it is vague: the model still has to guess what "serious" means. That doesn't show that writing things down fails.
  • D writes the line down: flag roof leaks (and the other named problems), skip routine notes such as weathered cable ties. So D fixes both problems in the stem: the routine notes now have a rule that says skip, and the leaks that were missed are now named.

Weak Rows Paper 1, 23 Oct, question 18

The pipeline flags a policy schedule for an account assistant when "something looks unusual". Of 600 flags last month, 41 were real problems, and assistants now skim past flags. The real problems were of 3 kinds: a premium above the quoted amount, a policy period that differs from the certificate, and a named insured that differs from the client record. What change cuts the false flags?

✗ Your pick: CAdd 5 examples of normal schedules for the model to compare each one against
✓ Correct: BFlag only on the 3 named conditions, with the differences to ignore listed
The other two options (A, D)
  • AHave the model score each schedule 1–10 for oddness and flag only 8 or above
  • DSend flags to the assistants in daily batches of 50, sorted oldest first
Where it went wrong: the stem hands you the list: the 3 kinds of real problem. When the stem names what counts, write it down as the rule (B). Your pick, 5 normal schedules, shows the model what "normal" looks like, but every real schedule differs from those 5 in many small ways. The model still has to decide which differences matter, which is the same guess that produced 559 false flags (600 flags, of which 41 were real). A doesn't help either: a 1–10 "oddness" score comes from the same vague sense of "unusual".

Weak Rows Paper 2, 23 Oct, question 13

The document-analysis subagent flags any figure in a paper that "looks inconsistent with our survey data". Of 420 flags last quarter, 290 turned out to be tonnes against kilograms, a different survey year or rounding. A prompt line saying "only flag discrepancies you are highly confident about" cut the flags by 5%. What change cuts the false flags?

✗ Your pick: CAdd 4 papers that agree with the survey data for the subagent to compare against
✓ Correct: BFlag only same species, area and year gaps over 10%, after converting units
The other two options (A, D)
  • AHave the subagent score each discrepancy 0–100 and pass on scores over 80
  • DLet the synthesis subagent drop the flagged figures it judges unimportant
Where it went wrong:
  • In option B, "gaps over 10%" means the paper's figure and the survey's figure differ by more than 10%.
  • The stem names the 3 causes of the false flags: units, survey year, rounding. B answers each one: compare like with like (same species, area and year), convert units first, and allow a margin for rounding.
  • Papers that agree with the survey data (your pick) show nothing about the differences that don't matter. A paper in kilograms still looks like it disagrees with a table in tonnes.
  • The prompt line that was tried, "only flag what you are highly confident about", is the vague kind. The exam guide names it as an instruction that does not improve precision.
  • You first picked B, then switched because 10% looked too big. Judge the method first: B was the only option that dealt with the 3 named causes. A number that looks a little off on the right method still beats the wrong method.

Part 3 · When examples are the right fix

Examples win in one situation: the cases are judgement calls that can't all be listed. The stem tells you by saying both of these:

  • Rules were already written, and they didn't fix it.
  • New cases keep turning up that the rules miss.

"Rules" here means a named list, like the 9 skip rules in the card below. A vague line such as "focus on serious issues" doesn't count: it names nothing, so it counts as no rules, and the fix is still to write criteria (Part 1).

Then you give 2 to 4 cases that sit right next to the line, on both sides, each with the reason it is one or the other. The model learns the reasoning and applies it to cases nobody listed. This is the "hard-case examples" row in the diagram.

One more thing from the exam guide, so this doesn't swing too far: criteria can carry examples. When a review sorts problems into levels such as high, medium and low (severity levels), the guide says to define each level and give a code example for each: for instance, "High: data can be lost. Example: deleting rows with no backup." The examples there explain the criteria; they don't replace them.

Weak Rows Paper 2, 23 Oct, question 3

The review flags "possible N+1 query" on 45% of pull requests, and developers dismiss 70% of those flags. Most dismissed flags are loops over records already loaded with includes earlier in the method, or loops over 2 or 3 fixed items. A list of 9 skip rules for N+1 flags was added last month; dismissals fell only to 64%, and each week reviewers find another harmless shape the rules miss. What should you add?

✗ Your pick: DA pause on N+1 flags until the skip list covers every harmless loop shape
✓ Correct: A4 code cases, each a harmless loop or a real N+1, with why it is which
The other two options (B, C)
  • BA tenth skip rule for loops that run over a fixed list of fewer than 4 items
  • CA 0–1 score on each N+1 flag, posting only flags that score above 0.8
Where it went wrong: here, unlike the three cards above, rules were written (9 of them) and barely moved the number (70% to 64%). And a new harmless loop turns up every week, so the list can never be finished. That is the sign for examples. Option A in plain words: put 4 short pieces of code in the prompt. Two are loops that look like an N+1 but are fine (the records were already loaded earlier; the loop runs over 3 fixed items). Two are real N+1s. Each one says why. From the reasons, the model learns the judgement and can apply it to shapes nobody listed. Pausing a noisy kind of flag is a real move (the exam guide lists it under 4.1), but only while you fix its prompt. D waits for a skip list that will never cover every shape, so the flags never come back.
The check: did the stem say rules were tried and new cases keep slipping past them? Then 2–4 examples near the line, with reasons. If no rules were tried and the stem names what counts, write the rules.

Weak Rows Paper 2, 23 Oct, question 8

Officers can check about 250 of the 3,000 documents processed each week. The model gives each extracted field a score from 0 to 1, and any document with a field under 0.9 goes to review: 1,400 a week. Spot checks find many wrong fields at 0.95 and many right ones at 0.7. Officers hand-checked every field on 1,800 documents last season. What should the team do?

✗ Your pick: DPut 5 low-scoring documents from the 1,800 in the prompt as examples
✓ Correct: CSet each field's cutoff from its error rate by score on the 1,800
The other two options (A, B)
  • AWrite a rubric telling the model when a field should get a low score
  • BLower the cutoff from 0.9 to 0.6 until the queue fits 250 a week
Where it went wrong: what is broken here is the scores: a 0.95 is often wrong and a 0.7 often right, so a cutoff of 0.9 means nothing yet. And the stem gives you an answer key: 1,800 documents a person already checked field by field. Calibrating (C) means: for each field, look at how often the model was wrong at each score on those 1,800, and set the cutoff at the score below which that field is wrong too often to trust. Then a score means a measured error rate. Your pick puts 5 of those documents in the prompt as examples. That might change how the model reads documents, but it never ties a score to an error rate, so the review cutoff is still a guess. B also just guesses: it picks the number that makes the queue fit 250, and the scores stay unreliable.

Weak Rows Paper 2, 23 Oct, question 17

A paper gives a cod stock biomass of 41,000 tonnes in its abstract and 14,000 tonnes in its results table. The subagent extracted 41,000 with a score of 0.97, above the 0.9 threshold calibrated on hand-checked figures, so it went straight into a quota briefing. In a review of 200 figures that skipped review, 6 of the 7 errors came from documents that gave 2 different values for the same figure. What should change?

✗ Your pick: ARe-calibrate the 0.9 threshold on a new labelled set that includes such papers
✓ Correct: DSend a figure to a scientist when its source gives 2 values, whatever its score
The other two options (B, C)
  • BHave the subagent take the results-table value whenever a paper gives 2 values
  • CWrite criteria telling the subagent when to lower its own score for a figure
Where it went wrong: what is broken here is the source document: the paper says 41,000 in one place and 14,000 in another. The model read 41,000 correctly, so its high score was honest. It is like copying a phone number perfectly from a page that has a typo in it: a better copier can't fix the page. Calibration (your pick) is already done, and re-doing it can't help: a correct copy of a wrong figure scores high, just as a correct copy of a right figure does, so no cutoff can tell them apart. The exam guide says to send contradictory source documents to a person. So D: when the source gives 2 values, a scientist decides, whatever the score.

Weak Rows Paper 2, 23 Oct, question 19

The 3 scientists can check 400 figures a month out of about 2,500 extracted, and every figure feeds a quota briefing. Field thresholds were calibrated last quarter on 1,500 hand-checked figures, and about 1,100 figures a month fall below them. A board member suggests the subagent's scores should not decide anything. How should the 400 checks be allocated?

✗ Your pick: AA random 400 of the 1,100, as a fair sample of the figures below threshold
✓ Correct: CThe 400 figures with the lowest calibrated scores, lowest first
The other two options (B, D)
  • BThe 400 figures for the species with the largest quotas this year
  • DExplicit criteria for the subagent to rank its own figures by doubt
Where it went wrong: nothing is broken here: the scores are already calibrated, so a low score really does mean a likely error. The question is only how to spend 400 checks. Send them where the errors are most likely: lowest calibrated score first (C). A random sample (your pick) is the tool for measuring how accurate the model is. It would spread the 400 checks evenly and leave many of the likely errors unchecked. The board member's suggestion is there to tempt you, and it is wrong: once scores are calibrated against hand-checked data, they are exactly what should decide the order.
The check: before the options, finish the sentence "What is broken is ___." The answer is one of: the rules; the cases the rules miss; the scores; the source document; nothing, there are just too few checks; or we haven't measured how accurate it is. Each one has its own row in the table.
