One message, or one at a time?

When Claude's work comes back with several problems, how you hand them back decides whether the next round fixes them or trades one for another.

The one question for every list of problems: does fixing one of them change another?
Yes → put them in one message, and say how they connect.
No → fix them one at a time, checking each before the next.

An everyday example. A plumber has just fitted your new kitchen. You find three problems:

  • The tap drips and the sink drains slowly. Both are in the same pipework under the sink. If you call about the drip on Monday and the drain on Thursday, the plumber opens up the same pipes twice. The Monday fix may even move the pipe that causes Thursday's problem. Tell them about both on the same call, and mention they're both under the sink.
  • A cupboard door sticks. It has nothing to do with the pipes. Fixing it can't change the tap, and fixing the tap can't change the door. Raise it on its own, whenever suits.

Same kitchen, three problems, two different ways to report them. The question isn't "how many problems?" It's "are they tangled together?"

Both picks split up problems that were tangled together. Splitting feels careful. Here it was the cause of the failure.

1 · How to tell they're tangled

Three signs in a stem tell you fixing one will change another:

SignExample from your misses
One problem's output is another's inputThe dedup key is built from the parsed date. A wrong date makes a wrong key.
They live in the same stepThe null-email drop and the key collision both happen in the dedup step. Any fix to one edits the code the other lives in.
Fixing one broke anotherSix date formats, five rounds, each round broke a format that had worked. When that happens, the problems are tangled.

The opposite signs mean they're independent: different files or features, no data passing between them, and no history of one fix breaking another. A misspelt button label and a slow image upload are a fair example.

Things that are not signs either way: the same reviewer found them, they were found on the same day, they're all "high severity", they're in the same repo. Exam distractors use these to make a split or a bundle sound reasonable.

2 · Why tangled problems go in one message

Claude can only plan around the problems it's told about. Give it the date bug alone, and it chooses a date fix without knowing the dedup key depends on the date. When you raise the dedup key next round, that first fix may need reworking, and each rework can disturb something that already worked. That's how you get fix-one-break-another.

One message lets Claude design one change that satisfies every problem at once. The guide says it directly: address "multiple interacting issues in a single detailed message when fixes interact".

A good message names each problem with a concrete case (lesson 13's input and expected output), then says how the problems connect:

// one message, three tangled problems
Three problems in the importer. They interact, so please fix them together.

1. Dates are parsed in UTC. They must use the account's timezone.
   Input:  "2026-10-04T02:00:00Z", account in America/New_York
   Expect: 2026-10-03 (10 pm local)   Got: 2026-10-04
2. The dedup key is built from the parsed date, so bug 1 makes late-evening
   rows collide with the next day's rows.
3. The same dedup step drops every row with a null email. Keep those rows.

Fixing 1 will change how 2 behaves, and 2 and 3 are in the same function.

The highlighted line is what the split approaches never send: how the problems connect.

3 · Why independent problems go one at a time

When the problems really are separate, the reasoning flips. One fix at a time keeps each change small and easy to check. If something breaks, you know which fix did it. The guide calls this "sequential iteration for independent issues". It fits the Claude Code best-practices advice to correct Claude early, because "the best results come from tight feedback loops".

So "one at a time" isn't wrong. It's wrong for tangled problems. That's what makes it such a good distractor: it's the right answer to a different stem.

4 · Traps and one exception, kept for last

Option you'll seeWhy it fails on tangled problems
"Fix the root bug first, then raise the others once it's verified"Sounds disciplined. But the root fix is chosen before Claude knows what depends on it, so the next round may have to rework it. It's still the split approach, just in order.
"Separate parallel sessions, one per problem, then merge"Each session edits the same step without seeing the others. You get conflicting diffs and no view of how the problems connect. Your D3 Q10 pick.
"Bundle everything into one message, always"Wrong the other way round. For independent problems, bundling throws away the small, checkable steps.

The exception: corrections are piling up. If you've already corrected Claude more than twice on the same issue, the best-practices guide says the context is "cluttered with failed approaches". Run /clear and start fresh with a more specific prompt that includes what you learned. For tangled problems, that fresh prompt is the one-message hand-back from section 2.

Explain it back

Answer both in chat before the quiz. Each one gives you everything you need.
  1. A shopping cart. Claude rewrote a shopping cart, and you found three problems:
    1. The discount is taken off after tax is added, when it should come off before.
    2. The "you saved $X" line on the receipt is worked out from the total after tax.
    3. The cart icon is the wrong colour.
    Part 1: which problems go together in one message, and which go on their own? (Answer shape: "Problems __ and __ together; problem __ on its own.")
    Part 2: which of the three signs told you? A: one problem's output is another's input. B: they happen in the same step. C: fixing one has already broken another. Say why in one sentence.
    Hint: try a $100 item, $10 off, 10% tax. Does fixing problem 1 change what problem 2 shows? Could it ever change the icon?
  2. Your own miss: D3 quiz, 16 Oct, Q10. The original question:
    Reviewing Claude's rewrite of a CSV importer, you find three problems. Dates are parsed in UTC instead of the account's timezone. The dedup key is built from the parsed date, and so late-evening rows collide with the next day's rows. Rows with a null email are also dropped by the dedup step. How should you hand these back?

    A) Fix the timezone bug first and raise the dedup problems after that change is verified
    B) Describe all three in one message, explaining how the date bug feeds the dedup key ✓ correct
    C) Open three separate sessions, one per problem, and then merge the three diffs together ✗ your pick
    D) Ask for a fresh rewrite of the importer, noting that dates and nulls need extra care
    A worked case: a New York customer buys at 10 pm on 3 October. In UTC that is 2 am on 4 October, so the importer records 4 October. The same customer buys again on the afternoon of 4 October, also recorded as 4 October. The dedup check (same email + same date = duplicate) keeps one and throws the other away.
    The ask: with your pick, one session is told only "late-evening rows collide with next-day rows". It doesn't know about the timezone bug. What would it probably change to stop the collision, and why does that clash with the timezone session's change when you merge?
